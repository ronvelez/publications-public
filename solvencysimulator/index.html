National Social Security and Medicare Hospital Insurance Data Simulator

This is a free educational tool. Its purpose is to show how the solvency of Social Security and Medicare hospital insurance could change for the whole country. A trust fund is the savings account for that program. Solvent means the account can keep paying full promised benefits. This is a teaching tool, not a projection from the Social Security Administration or Medicare.

You choose the full retirement age and the other eligibility ages. You can add a possible yearly transfer from other federal funds. You can also add tariffs on earned income above $10,000,000. On this page, a tariff is an extra charge on that pay. It is not a tax on imported goods. Those choices together set the payroll tax rates on the income tiers. A tier is a band of earnings. The Solvency Simulation Summary Report and the charts then show how long each savings account would last.

The simulator uses only earned income, as the IRS defines it: wages, salaries, tips, and net self-employment earnings. Interest, dividends, capital gains, pensions, and other unearned income are left out. See earned income at IRS.gov.

Under today’s law, the worker and the employer together pay 12.4% of wages for Social Security, up to $184,500 in 2026. Wages above that pay no Social Security tax. They together pay 2.9% of all wages for Medicare hospital insurance. Only the worker pays an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (joint). Those dollar limits do not rise with wages.

This is not a personal Social Security or Medicare estimate, and it does not calculate anyone’s own benefits. For a personal account, visit my Social Security.

    Payroll tax to employer-paid health insurance (optional)

    This choice is not Current Law. It is a proposed option. Leave it unchecked for today’s rules. The tax is on the premium the employer pays to the health insurance company for a worker’s plan. That is the employer’s share of the company health plan. If a family plan costs $27,000 and the employer pays $20,000 of it, the 2% is charged on that $20,000, not on the $7,000 the worker already pays. The worker’s own premium payment is not taxed again. Cash wages, deductibles, copays, and doctor bills are not taxed by this charge. The health insurance company is not taxed. The 2% on the employer’s premium is then split evenly: the worker pays 1%, withheld from the paycheck, and the employer pays 1%. On that $20,000 example, the new charge is about $400 in total and the worker’s piece is about $200 a year. Check the box to deposit the estimate into the Medicare hospital-insurance trust fund each year. The estimate uses about $1.05 trillion of employer-paid premiums. Two percent of that is $21 billion a year. Hospital insurance receives all $21 billion. Social Security and disability receive none. The money does not raise benefits and it does not change the wage-tax rates. The amount is in 2026 dollars, rises with prices, and is paid each year through 2100. The dates above move as soon as you check it. The dashed line is Current Law and leaves this money out.

    Slower yearly benefit increase (optional)

    This choice is not Current Law. It is a proposed option. Leave it unchecked for today’s rules. The yearly Social Security raise would use the chained consumer price index instead of today’s price index. Today, a Social Security check goes up once a year with the consumer price index for wage earners. The chained index has risen about 0.3 percentage points less per year. On a $1,000 check, next year’s raise would be about $3 smaller. The gap compounds. After about 10 years the monthly check is roughly 3% smaller than under today’s raise, and after about 20 years it is roughly 6% smaller. The first check a person receives stays the same, because that starting benefit is based on wages, not on this yearly raise. People already getting benefits receive the smaller raise too. About one in twenty people on the rolls is new each year, so the full effect builds as older checks are replaced. The smaller raise starts with the December adjustment of the “Takes effect January 1” year. With that year at 2027, the first smaller checks are paid in 2028. Retirement, survivors, and disability all use this raise. Medicare hospital insurance does not. Hospital costs stay on today’s path. Combined Social Security’s date moves when you check it. The early-2030s change is small, because one year of a smaller raise does not shrink the checks much yet. By 2100 the smaller raises have compounded, so income covers a larger share of those reduced checks. The dashed line is Current Law and keeps today’s raise.

    What this tool does and does not do Current Law

    What it does

    • Shows the retirement account, the disability account, Social Security as a whole, and the Medicare hospital account from 2026 through 2100.
    • Compares your package with Current Law on the same charts. Current Law is the dashed line.
    • Shows how taxes, income bands, and eligibility ages move the year the account runs out, the savings compared with one year of cost, and the share of promised benefits that incoming money can still pay.

    What it does not do

    • It is not an official score. A full official estimate uses detailed population, wage, and claiming patterns that this page simplifies.
    • It does not model Medicare Part B (doctor visits) or Part D (prescriptions), private premiums, Medicaid, employer coverage, or family hospital bills.
    • It does not change benefits for people already getting them. It also does not show poverty, retirement decisions, or the federal budget beyond these savings accounts.

    Methodology

    The starting point is the middle official projection for 2026. Under Current Law, with no extra federal transfer, combined Social Security runs out in the third quarter of 2034. Income would then cover about 83% of promised benefits, falling toward 65% by 2100. Retirement and survivors run out in the fourth quarter of 2032 (about 78%, then 62% by 2100). Disability Insurance lasts through 2100. Medicare hospital insurance runs out in the second quarter of 2033 (about 89%, about 85% in 2050, and about 93% by 2100). If the transfer box is above 0, the cards show a later date than these.

    The savings at the end of 2025 were $2,338 billion for retirement and survivors, $223 billion for disability, and $256 billion for hospital insurance. Tax money and cost follow smoothed paths through the published rates. Social Security tax money, not counting interest, is 12.91% of taxed wages in 2026 and 13.45% in 2100. The cost of promised benefits is 15.37% in 2026, 20.45% around 2085, and 20.02% in 2100. Hospital insurance tax money goes from 3.49% to 4.51% of its taxed wages, and cost from 3.49% through 4.54% in 2045 to 4.86% in 2100. An interest rate of 3.5% and wage growth of about 3.6% are chosen so this simpler picture runs out in those same quarters. The share of promised benefits that incoming money can still pay lands within about two points of the official figures.

    The 2026 wage base, the cap on the Social Security tax, is $184,500. About 83% of Social Security wages are treated as falling under that cap. That share is an assumption. It fits with hospital-insurance wages averaging about 25% larger than Social Security wages, because hospital insurance has no cap. Income bands apply the rates you set to that pattern. Freezing the bands holds them in 2026 dollars while wages grow about 3.6% a year.

    Program size comes from the 2026 official summary. At the end of 2025, 62.3 million people received retirement and survivor benefits, 8.2 million received disability benefits, and 69.3 million people were in Medicare. About 184.7 million people paid Social Security taxes and 188.7 million paid Medicare taxes. Savings compared with one year of cost were 153% for retirement and survivors, 132% for disability, and 53% for hospital insurance at the start of 2026. Hospital insurance enrollment in that report was 62.0 million older people and 7.1 million people with disabilities in 2025. The 2.5% cost change per year of eligibility age is a rough stand-in for one young age group, not an official Medicare estimate.

    Each year the full retirement age rises, a new retiree’s promised benefit is about 6.7% lower if they claim at the old full retirement age. About one-twentieth of retired people are replaced by new awards each year, so the savings account feels the change slowly. The earliest claiming age has a small long-run effect, because a later claim comes with a higher monthly benefit, plus a near-term dip while those claims are delayed. The long-range hospital cost path is smoothed so Current Law still runs out in the second quarter of 2033. The official cost rate reaches 4.99% of taxed wages around 2085 before easing to 4.86% in 2100.
